CPAP therapy and dry mouth: tips for better sleep

Why CPAP therapy can cause dry mouth, with humidifier adjustments, mask-fit tips, hydration habits and when to seek support.

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy can improve breathing and sleep for people with sleep apnea. However, some users wake up with a dry, sticky, or uncomfortable mouth. Dry mouth is a recognized side effect of CPAP therapy, but it does not necessarily mean you need to stop treatment. In many cases, identifying the cause and making a few adjustments can improve comfort.

Why does CPAP therapy cause dry mouth?

CPAP machines deliver a steady flow of pressurized air to help keep the airway open during sleep. Although this airflow is essential to treatment, it can contribute to dryness—especially when air escapes through the mouth.

Common causes include:

  • Sleeping with your mouth open: Mouth breathing allows moisture to evaporate more quickly.
  • Mask leaks: Air escaping around the mask or through the mouth can increase dryness and may reduce the effectiveness of treatment.
  • Low humidification: Air that is not adequately humidified may dry the mouth, nose, and throat.
  • Nasal congestion: A blocked or stuffy nose can make mouth breathing more likely.
  • Medications and health conditions: Certain medications and medical conditions can reduce saliva production, making CPAP-related dryness feel more severe.
  • Dehydration: Not drinking enough fluids throughout the day may contribute to nighttime discomfort.

The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute lists dry mouth and nasal symptoms among the possible side effects of CPAP therapy and recommends discussing persistent issues with a health professional.

Review your humidifier settings

Many CPAP machines include a built-in or attachable humidifier that adds moisture to the air before it reaches your airway. If you regularly wake up with a dry mouth, your humidification level may need attention.

Try these tips:

  • Confirm that the humidifier is filled, connected, and functioning correctly.
  • Gradually increase the humidity setting according to the manufacturer’s instructions.
  • Consider using heated tubing if it is compatible with your machine.
  • Monitor the tubing for condensation, sometimes called “rainout,” which may indicate that the humidity or tube temperature needs adjustment.
  • Clean the humidifier chamber regularly and use the type of water recommended by the equipment manufacturer.

A heated humidifier and heated tubing may help reduce CPAP-related dryness. Because machines vary, review your device instructions or speak with your sleep-care team before making significant changes.

Check your mask fit

A mask that does not fit correctly can allow air to leak throughout the night. This may cause dry mouth, interrupt sleep, and interfere with effective therapy.

To improve your mask fit:

  • Put the mask on while lying in your usual sleeping position.
  • Adjust the straps evenly without overtightening them.
  • Check the cushion for wear, cracks, stiffness, or buildup.
  • Clean the mask according to the manufacturer’s directions.
  • Replace worn mask components as recommended.
  • Review your machine’s leak information, if available.

Mask style also matters. People who breathe through their mouths while using a nasal mask may benefit from discussing a chin strap or a full-face mask with their sleep clinic. A full-face mask covers both the nose and mouth and may help some users manage mouth leaks and dryness.

Do not change your pressure settings on your own. If the airflow feels too strong, the mask frequently leaks, or dryness continues despite basic adjustments, contact your sleep specialist or equipment provider.

Stay hydrated throughout the day

Good hydration supports normal saliva production and may help reduce the severity of dry-mouth symptoms.

Consider the following habits:

  • Drink water regularly throughout the day.
  • Keep water nearby for occasional sips when needed.
  • Limit excessive caffeine and alcohol, particularly later in the day.
  • Avoid tobacco products, which can worsen oral dryness.
  • Use a room humidifier if the air in your bedroom is especially dry.
  • Maintain a consistent oral-hygiene routine.

While sipping water can provide temporary comfort, it may not provide lasting relief for chronic dry mouth. Drinking large amounts immediately before bed may also cause sleep interruptions for bathroom trips.

When should you contact a health professional?

Speak with your doctor or sleep-care team if:

  • Dry mouth remains severe or persistent.
  • Your mask frequently leaks or feels uncomfortable.
  • You regularly remove the mask while sleeping.
  • Nasal congestion makes CPAP use difficult.
  • You develop mouth sores, difficulty swallowing, or changes in taste.
  • Dryness begins after starting a new medication.
  • You are unable to use your CPAP consistently.

Persistent dry mouth can affect more than comfort. Saliva helps protect the teeth and oral tissues, so ongoing dryness may increase the risk of cavities, irritation, and other oral-health concerns.

Better comfort can support better sleep

Dry mouth is a common CPAP challenge, but it is often manageable. Reviewing humidifier settings, correcting mask leaks, staying hydrated, and using an appropriate dry-mouth product may make therapy more comfortable.

Keep using your CPAP as directed by your sleep clinic, and work with your doctor, dentist, or equipment supplier to find the combination of solutions that works best for you.
