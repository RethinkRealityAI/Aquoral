Medications

Medications that can cause dry mouth

Which medications contribute to dry mouth, how to spot the pattern, and how to protect your oral health and comfort without stopping treatment.

Understanding which medications contribute to xerostomia is essential for preventing long-term oral health complications and staying comfortable.

Dry mouth is a common side effect of many prescription and over-the-counter medicines. Some drugs reduce the signals that stimulate the salivary glands, while others may affect fluid balance, breathing, or the amount and quality of saliva.

The likelihood and severity of dryness vary by the individual medicine, dose, timing, health history and number of medicines used. A medication class can be associated with dry mouth without every drug, or every person, being affected.

Key takeaway: If dry mouth begins or worsens after a medication change, note the pattern and discuss it with your doctor, pharmacist or dentist. Do not stop treatment on your own.

Why medicines can make the mouth feel dry

  • Reduced gland stimulation: Some medicines block nerve signals involved in saliva production.
  • Fluid-balance changes: Certain medicines increase urination or affect hydration, which may worsen oral dryness in some people.
  • Thicker saliva: The amount or consistency of saliva may change even when the glands still produce some fluid.
  • Mouth breathing: Medicines that contribute to congestion, sedation or altered sleep may indirectly influence nighttime symptoms.
  • Combined effects: Taking several medicines with drying properties can increase the overall burden.

Patterns worth recording

  • Symptoms after a dose: Dryness follows a predictable daily schedule. Record the dose time and when symptoms begin.
  • Symptoms after a new medicine: Dryness starts soon after starting it. Record the start date and other new symptoms.
  • Symptoms after a dose increase: Dryness becomes more intense. Record the old and new dose, if known.
  • Cumulative effects: Dryness increases as medicines are added. Keep a complete list of prescription and over-the-counter medicines.
  • Nighttime symptoms: Dryness is worst during sleep or on waking. Record bedtime medicines, breathing and CPAP use.

1. Antidepressants

Dry mouth is reported with multiple types of antidepressants. Tricyclic antidepressants often have stronger anticholinergic effects, which can reduce salivary stimulation. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) and other antidepressants may also cause oral dryness in some people.

What to discuss with your doctor

  • When the dryness began compared with starting or increasing the medicine
  • Whether symptoms are mild, persistent, or interfering with eating, speaking or sleep
  • Other side effects such as dizziness, constipation, blurred vision or sedation
  • All other medicines and supplements that may add drying effects
  • Whether a safe timing, dose, formulation or treatment adjustment is appropriate

Mental-health safety: Do not abruptly stop an antidepressant. Sudden discontinuation can cause withdrawal symptoms and may worsen the condition being treated. Any change should be planned with your doctor.

2. Blood-pressure medications

Medicines used to treat high blood pressure include several different classes, and dry mouth is not equally common with all of them. Diuretics may contribute through fluid loss, while some centrally acting agents, beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, ACE inhibitors or other cardiovascular medicines may be associated with oral dryness in certain people.

Because blood-pressure control protects the heart, brain and kidneys, treatment should not be skipped because of dry mouth. Your doctor can evaluate whether the medicine, dehydration, another condition or a combination of factors is responsible.

  • Did dryness start after a new blood-pressure medicine or dose change? Timing helps identify a possible medication relationship.
  • Are there fluid restrictions or heart or kidney concerns? Generic advice to “drink more water” may not be safe for everyone.
  • Are dizziness, weakness or reduced urination present? These symptoms may need prompt medical review.
  • Are several drying medicines being used? The combined medication burden may matter more than one drug.

Cardiovascular safety: Never stop a blood-pressure medicine or diuretic without medical guidance. Ask your care team how to manage dryness while keeping your heart treatment safe.

3. Antihistamines

Antihistamines are used for allergies, itching, motion sickness and other symptoms. Many can dry the mouth by blocking histamine and, depending on the product, anticholinergic pathways. Older, sedating antihistamines may be especially drying, but newer products can also cause symptoms in some people.

Combination cold and allergy products may include an antihistamine plus a decongestant or another drying ingredient. Reading the product label and avoiding duplicate ingredients can help prevent unintentional stacking.

Questions for a pharmacist

  • Which active ingredients are in the product?
  • Is the antihistamine sedating or strongly drying?
  • Am I taking another product with the same ingredient?
  • Could a non-drug allergy strategy or different formulation be appropriate?
  • Could the medicine interact with my other medicines or health conditions?

4. Other common culprits

  • Decongestants (nasal congestion and cold symptoms) may reduce secretions and worsen dryness.
  • Bladder-control medicines (overactive bladder and urinary urgency): many have anticholinergic effects.
  • Pain medicines (acute or chronic pain): some can reduce saliva or cause sedation and mouth breathing.
  • Anxiety or sleep medicines: sedation and anticholinergic effects may contribute.
  • Muscle relaxants (muscle spasm): some have drying or sedating properties.
  • Respiratory medicines (asthma and COPD): some inhaled or oral medicines may contribute; inhalers can also affect oral tissues.
  • Seizure medicines (epilepsy and neurological conditions): dry mouth can occur with certain agents.
  • Parkinson’s or nausea medicines: anticholinergic activity may reduce secretions.
  • Cancer therapies: chemotherapy and immunotherapy may alter saliva; head-and-neck radiation can injure glands.

This list is not complete. Hundreds of medicines may contribute to dry mouth. The side-effect profile depends on the specific drug, dose, combination and person, not just the broad category.

Prescription and over-the-counter medicines both matter

A medication review should include more than prescriptions. Allergy tablets, sleep aids, cold products, pain relievers, supplements, inhalers, patches and as-needed medicines can all affect the overall picture.

Bring a complete medication list

  • Medicine name and strength
  • How much you take and how often
  • Time of day each dose is taken
  • Reason for use
  • Prescription, over-the-counter, supplement or as-needed status
  • Approximate start date or recent dose change
  • Dry mouth timing and severity

Signs of medication-induced dry mouth

  • Sticky, dry, rough or burning feeling
  • Thick or stringy saliva
  • Frequent thirst or waking for water
  • Cracked lips or mouth sores
  • Difficulty chewing, swallowing, tasting or speaking
  • Bad breath or unpleasant taste
  • Denture rubbing or poor retention
  • New cavities, sensitivity or oral infections

Persistent medication-induced dry mouth can increase the risk of tooth decay and fungal infection because saliva helps cleanse the mouth, buffer acids, strengthen teeth and control harmful organisms.

How your care team may approach the problem

  • 1. Confirm the pattern: Review symptom timing, oral findings, hydration, medical conditions, breathing and salivary function.
  • 2. Review every medicine: Check for drying effects, duplicate ingredients, interactions and cumulative anticholinergic burden.
  • 3. Protect oral health: Use fluoride, daily plaque control, dietary guidance and risk-based dental visits.
  • 4. Relieve symptoms: Consider water, sugar-free stimulation, humidification and saliva substitutes as appropriate.
  • 5. Reassess: Track whether symptoms and oral findings improve after the care plan changes.

A medication change is not always needed. If a medicine is essential or alternatives are unsuitable, the plan may focus on oral prevention and symptom control while continuing treatment.

Practical ways to manage dry mouth

  • Sip fluids safely: Use water regularly when medically appropriate; follow any fluid restriction from your doctor.
  • Protect teeth: Brush twice daily with fluoride toothpaste and clean between teeth every day.
  • Reduce sugar and acid frequency: Frequent snacks, sweet drinks, candies and acidic beverages can increase cavity risk.
  • Stimulate saliva when safe: Sugar-free gum or lozenges may help if glands still work and chewing or swallowing is safe.
  • Avoid aggravators: Tobacco, alcohol and some alcohol-containing mouthrinses may worsen irritation.
  • Use humidification: A clean bedroom humidifier may help with overnight dryness.
  • Schedule dental care: Ask whether more frequent exams or additional fluoride are appropriate.

Medication-related dry mouth at night

Bedtime doses, sedating medicines, reduced nighttime saliva, mouth breathing, nasal congestion, sleep apnea and CPAP mask leak can overlap. This may make dry mouth at night particularly noticeable.

When dry mouth and sleep affect one another, or dry mouth interrupts sleep repeatedly, record bedtime medications and breathing symptoms. CPAP dry mouth and sleep apnea dry mouth should be reviewed with your sleep-care team rather than managed by stopping CPAP.

CPAP safety: Do not stop CPAP therapy or change pressure settings because of dryness. Ask about mask fit, mouth leak, nasal symptoms and humidification.

Other causes still need consideration

Not every case of dryness is medication-related. Dehydration, diabetes, Sjögren’s disease, mouth breathing, tobacco or alcohol use, nerve injury, salivary-gland disorders and cancer treatment may contribute. Sjögren’s dry mouth may occur with dry eyes, fatigue, joint symptoms or gland swelling and can require coordinated medical and dental care.

When to seek professional help

  • Dryness that persists, worsens, or begins after a new medicine or dose change
  • Difficulty swallowing, choking, dehydration or unexplained weight loss
  • White or red patches, burning, sores or recurrent oral infection
  • New or rapidly increasing cavities or tooth sensitivity
  • Bleeding, swollen or painful gums
  • Severe dizziness, weakness, confusion, reduced urination, swelling or other concerning symptoms
  • Loud snoring, witnessed breathing pauses, or trouble tolerating CPAP

The bottom line

Antidepressants, blood-pressure medicines, antihistamines and many other common drugs can contribute to dry mouth, but the effect varies by product and person. A careful timeline and complete medication list help your care team identify likely contributors.

The safest plan protects the benefit of your treatment while reducing oral complications and discomfort. Never stop a medicine on your own; work with your doctor, pharmacist and dentist to review options and build a personal dry mouth plan.
