Sjögren’s-related dry mouth: effective relief and treatment options

How Sjögren’s causes dry mouth, the oral health effects, and how a lipid-based spray compares with saliva stimulants and water-based products.

Sjögren’s often causes persistent dry mouth (xerostomia) by damaging the salivary glands and reducing protective saliva. That loss of lubrication makes chewing, swallowing and tasting harder and weakens the mouth’s natural defences. This article explains how autoimmune salivary gland dysfunction produces xerostomia, reviews the oral health consequences, and outlines practical ways to find relief. You’ll learn how local lipid-based sprays differ from saliva-stimulating medicines, everyday management tips for Sjögren’s dry mouth, and how Aquoral’s lipid-based Oxidized Glycerol Triesters (OGT) technology can deliver hours of lubrication and protection.

What causes dry mouth in Sjögren’s, and what does it do to oral health?

Dry mouth in Sjögren’s is driven by an autoimmune attack: immune cells infiltrate and damage salivary gland tissue, which lowers both the amount of saliva and the protective factors that keep the mouth lubricated and microbes in check. Lower flow and changes in saliva quality increase friction, make it harder to form food into a ball for swallowing, and weaken natural defences against bacteria and fungi. This is why targeted local treatments that restore lubrication and protect the mouth can complement systemic treatments.

How does Sjögren’s produce xerostomia?

In Sjögren’s, lymphocytes infiltrate the salivary glands and progressively damage the cells that make saliva, causing lower output and altered composition. These changes reduce lubricating mucins and antimicrobial proteins, which lowers saliva’s suppleness and its ability to neutralise pathogens. As saliva volume falls, secretions can become thicker, making food harder to swallow and dulling taste. Local protective sprays that recreate a lipid layer can ease symptoms without exposing the rest of the body.

This autoimmune condition primarily affects the exocrine glands and commonly leads to oral symptoms such as xerostomia; current therapies remain largely palliative.

Sjögren’s: oral manifestations and palliative treatment. Sjögren’s is a systemic autoimmune disorder affecting roughly 4 million Americans, most often peri-menopausal women. It causes dysfunction and destruction of exocrine glands, producing oral and ocular symptoms — xerostomia and keratitis sicca among them. The condition is frequently undiagnosed for years. Diagnosis follows established criteria covering oral and ocular symptoms, clinical signs, evidence of gland involvement, and serology or biopsy. There is no definitive cure; management focuses on supportive and palliative measures. With an aging population and wider use of medications that promote dry mouth, the burden of xerostomia is increasing.

What are the common symptoms and oral complications?

People with Sjögren’s-related dry mouth commonly describe persistent oral dryness, trouble chewing or swallowing, changes in taste, cracked or tender tissue, and difficulty speaking for long periods. These problems reduce quality of life and can affect nutrition. Lower saliva also raises the risk of tooth decay, enamel erosion, thinning of the mouth lining and opportunistic infections such as candidiasis, so targeted prevention is essential.

  • Dry, sticky mouth that makes speaking more effortful and increases friction.
  • Harder chewing and swallowing, especially with dry or crumbly foods.
  • Taste changes and a higher risk of tooth decay and oral infections.

Salivary changes seen in some people with primary Sjögren’s, including altered salt handling, may reflect autoimmune gland dysfunction and could serve as clinical markers.

Salivary changes and dental caries are common for those with Sjögren’s. The salivary alterations seen in some patients with primary Sjögren’s suggest impaired ductal salt reabsorption while acinar transport may remain relatively preserved despite low secretion. These changes in flow and composition, together with increased dental caries, could serve as markers of the extent of autoimmune salivary-gland dysfunction. The findings also hint that ductal epithelium can be functionally affected in certain patients, meriting further pathophysiological study.

How Aquoral compares with other treatments for Sjögren’s dry mouth

Choosing a dry mouth treatment means balancing local lubrication and protection against saliva stimulation and convenience. Aquoral fills the local, lipid-based role. Saliva-stimulating medicines such as pilocarpine and cevimeline boost saliva by activating cholinergic pathways and can work when some gland function remains, but side effects and health conditions that rule them out limit their use for some people. Water-based saliva substitutes and gels offer short-term moisture but lack a persistent lipid barrier and need more frequent reapplication.
