Ageing

Why saliva production can decline with age: causes, symptoms and what helps

Why saliva can decrease as we get older, how medications and health conditions play a part, and practical steps to protect oral health.

Many people notice less saliva as they get older, a condition called xerostomia, or dry mouth. Below we explain why saliva production may fall with age, how medications and medical conditions factor in, and practical steps to protect oral health. You’ll find clear, evidence-informed information on the ageing–dry mouth link, common medication culprits, typical symptoms and oral risks, and effective management options.

Is dry mouth a normal part of ageing or caused by other factors?

Dry mouth is commonly associated with getting older, but ageing alone isn’t always the main cause. Healthy older adults often maintain normal saliva flow, though the glands can change with time. Other contributors like medications, chronic illness, and lifestyle choices often explain why some people develop persistent dryness. Distinguishing normal ageing from modifiable causes helps guide treatment.

How does ageing affect salivary gland function and saliva production?

With age, salivary glands can show structural changes and shifts in saliva composition that affect lubrication and mouth comfort. In many healthy seniors, overall flow remains within typical ranges, but the protective qualities of saliva may change. Since saliva supports digestion, oral hygiene, and infection defense, even subtle changes can matter for daily comfort and dental health.

What age-related conditions and lifestyle factors contribute to dry mouth?

Several health conditions and lifestyle factors that become more common with age can reduce saliva. Diabetes can interfere with saliva production, and autoimmune diseases such as Sjögren’s directly affect the salivary glands. Smoking, regular alcohol use, and not drinking enough fluids also make symptoms worse. Identifying these factors is an important first step toward better control.

Which medications commonly cause dry mouth in seniors?

Medications are a major cause of dry mouth, especially in older adults who often take multiple medicines. Knowing which drugs are most likely to reduce saliva helps doctors, pharmacists and patients find safer alternatives or management strategies.

The combination of an ageing population and higher medication use means medication-related dry mouth is an increasing public-health concern.

What are the most frequent drug classes leading to xerostomia in older adults?

Several drug classes are commonly linked with dry mouth in older adults. These include:

  • Antidepressants: Many have anticholinergic effects that lower saliva production.
  • Antihistamines: Widely used for allergies, these often cause mouth dryness.
  • Diuretics: Used for blood pressure or fluid control, they can reduce body hydration and saliva.

Other frequently implicated groups include antipsychotics, certain antihypertensives, and muscle relaxants, all of which can have xerogenic (drying) effects.

Evidence consistently links anticholinergic medication burden with higher rates of dry mouth in older adults.

Anticholinergic drugs and xerostomia in older adults. Systematic reviews show a clear association between anticholinergic drug use (or cumulative anticholinergic burden) and reported dry mouth among the elderly, reinforcing the need to review medication lists when symptoms appear.

Source: Association between anticholinergic activity and xerostomia and/or xerophthalmia in the elderly: systematic review, 2022.

How does polypharmacy increase the risk of reduced saliva production?

Polypharmacy, taking several medications at once, raises dry-mouth risk because different drugs’ anticholinergic or drying effects add up. That cumulative impact can cause persistent discomfort and functional problems like trouble swallowing or speaking. Doctors, nurses, and pharmacists can reduce risk by regularly reviewing medications and considering alternatives with lower xerogenic potential.

What are the common symptoms and oral health impacts of dry mouth in older adults?

Typical symptoms of dry mouth in older adults include a persistent sensation of dryness, thick or sticky saliva, and difficulty swallowing or speaking. Left unaddressed, these symptoms can reduce comfort, make eating harder, and lower quality of life.

How to recognize dry mouth symptoms like sticky mouth and difficulty swallowing

Recognizing dry mouth early makes treatment simpler. Common signs are:

  • A constant feeling of a dry mouth
  • Sticky, thick, or ropey saliva
  • Difficulty chewing, swallowing or articulating speech

If you or a loved one notices these symptoms, see a healthcare professional for a focused assessment and plan.

What are the long-term oral health risks associated with xerostomia in seniors?

Chronic reduced saliva increases the risk of tooth decay (caries), painful mucosal injuries, oral fungal infections such as candidiasis, and swallowing problems. Over time these issues can contribute to poor nutrition and a decline in overall well-being, so proactive management is important.

The combined effect of medication-induced dry mouth and polypharmacy creates complex, persistent oral-health challenges for older people.

Medication-induced dry mouth and polypharmacy in older patients. Medication-related xerostomia and reduced saliva will increasingly affect older patients due to high medication use and polypharmacy, with knock-on impacts such as dysphagia, higher caries rates, malnutrition, and poorer quality of life.

Source: Medication-induced xerostomia and hyposalivation in the elderly: culprits, complications, and management, 2018.

How can age-related dry mouth be effectively managed and treated?

Age-related dry mouth is manageable with a combination of professional care and everyday measures. Treatment focuses on identifying causes, protecting oral tissues, and restoring comfort and function.

  • Speech-language pathologists: Assess swallowing, teach compensatory strategies, and recommend saliva substitutes to ease mucosal dryness.
  • Rheumatologists: Diagnose autoimmune causes like Sjögren’s and coordinate systemic treatment while addressing oral symptoms.
  • Nurses: Spot medication-related xerostomia, educate patients, and support symptom-management routines.
  • Oncology support: For people with radiation- or chemotherapy-related xerostomia, targeted prevention and mucosal protection are essential.

Working together, these professionals balance disease treatment with practical steps to reduce oral complications and preserve quality of life.

What lifestyle adjustments help increase saliva production naturally?

Simple habits can ease dry-mouth symptoms:

  • Stay hydrated: Sip water regularly throughout the day to keep oral tissues moist.
  • Chew sugar-free gum: Chewing stimulates saliva flow and gives temporary relief.
  • Avoid caffeine and alcohol: Both can worsen dehydration and increase dryness.
