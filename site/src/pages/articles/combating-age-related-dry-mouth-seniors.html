Combating age-related dry mouth: a senior’s guide to relief

Causes and symptoms of dry mouth in older adults, practical home measures, and how a lipid-based spray can help.

What causes dry mouth in seniors and how is it identified?

Dry mouth in older adults usually has multiple causes: autoimmune disease, radiation therapy, and a range of commonly used medicines. Dentists and hygienists identify xerostomia through focused assessments, including symptom checklists, inspection for reduced salivary pooling or visible mucosal dryness, and monitoring for increased caries. Those evaluations clarify severity and likely contributors, guiding an appropriate care plan.

Increasing rates of dry mouth among older adults are a public health concern and are often compounded by polypharmacy as people age.

Geriatric xerostomia: polypharmacy and oral health challenges. The global population is aging, and with that shift comes more chronic illness and multimorbidity. These trends bring two closely linked problems for older adults: widespread polypharmacy and a rising burden of xerostomia. Together, they increase oral health risks and interfere with everyday function.

Which medications most often cause dry mouth in older adults?

Certain medications, often described as xerogenic agents, are frequent contributors to dry mouth in older people. Common culprits include drugs used for hypertension, depression, allergies and urinary incontinence. Identifying these medications is a key step toward individualized, effective xerostomia management.

Evidence from clinical practice emphasizes medication-induced dry mouth as a growing issue and supports tailored management strategies.

Medication-induced xerostomia in the elderly: management and saliva substitutes. As medication use rises among older patients, hyposalivation and xerostomia become more common. These problems can worsen swallowing difficulty, increase caries risk, contribute to poor nutrition, and reduce quality of life. Practical management includes switching to less xerogenic drugs or lowering doses when safe, and using a multidisciplinary, prevention-focused approach. Topical agents and saliva substitutes can provide symptomatic relief but should be chosen and adapted to each patient’s needs.

— Medication-induced xerostomia and hyposalivation in the elderly: culprits, complications, and management, 2018

Other factors also matter. For example, awareness of the oral effects of cannabis use is increasingly important when assessing and managing dry mouth.

What are the key symptoms of xerostomia in seniors?

Common symptoms include the sensation of a dry mouth, sticky or thick saliva, difficulty speaking or swallowing, and reduced oral comfort, all of which can diminish daily quality of life. Early recognition of these signs enables timely, targeted interventions.

Best remedies and lifestyle adjustments for senior dry mouth

Managing dry mouth effectively usually combines simple home measures with professional care. Together, these steps can ease symptoms and protect oral health over time.

How do hydration and humidifiers help?

Practical home strategies include staying well hydrated and using a humidifier, especially at night. Regular fluid intake helps maintain oral moisture, and a bedroom humidifier can reduce environmental dryness and lessen overnight symptoms.

Recommended dietary changes and oral hygiene practices

  • Dietary changes: Dental hygienists and dentists suggest diets and routine habits that lower the risk of cavities while protecting fragile oral tissues.
  • Oral hygiene practices:
    • Regular screening for dry mouth, sticky saliva, and swallowing difficulty.
    • Clear, practical oral hygiene instructions: gentle brushing, regular interdental cleaning, and appropriate fluoride use.
    • Behavioural coaching to conserve saliva and reduce decay risk.
    • Topical fluoride treatments and advice to choose non‑erosive products that protect vulnerable mucosa.
    • Ongoing symptom monitoring and reinforcement of hydration and saliva‑preserving habits.

When combined with professional advice, these measures help control symptoms and protect long-term oral health.

Where seniors can find treatment and support

Seniors benefit most from coordinated, multidisciplinary care involving dental hygienists, dentists, speech‑language pathologists, rheumatologists, oncology teams and nursing staff. These professionals provide practical screening, targeted therapies and guidance on saliva substitutes. Nursing support also helps with consistent use and comfort for people living with xerostomia.
