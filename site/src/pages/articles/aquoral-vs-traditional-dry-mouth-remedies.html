Aquoral oral spray vs traditional dry mouth remedies

How Aquoral’s lipid-based formulation compares with lozenges, gels and rinses for relieving dry mouth.

Xerostomia, commonly called dry mouth, is a common condition that can significantly affect quality of life. It happens when salivary glands produce too little saliva, causing oral discomfort and problems with speech, swallowing, and taste. This article reviews the causes and clinical features of xerostomia, evaluates Aquoral Protective Oral Spray as a saliva substitute, and compares its performance with conventional remedies to help support informed management choices.

What are the primary causes and symptoms of dry mouth?

Xerostomia can result from medications, systemic disease, or cancer treatments. Identifying the underlying cause is important for targeted and effective management.

How do CPAP therapy, cancer treatments, and medications contribute to dry mouth?

CPAP therapy, used for obstructive sleep apnea, can increase evaporative loss inside the mouth and worsen dry mouth. Cancer treatments, especially radiotherapy to the head and neck, may damage salivary glands and reduce saliva production. Certain medications, including antihistamines and some antidepressants, are well known to decrease salivary flow.

What are the clinical manifestations and severity levels of xerostomia?

Clinical signs include persistent oral dryness, difficulty swallowing, and a higher risk of dental cavities and mucosal problems. Severity depends on the cause: some people have mild symptoms while others face significant functional difficulties. Early recognition and treatment reduce the risk of secondary complications.

Aquoral forms a protective lipid layer on the oral mucosa. Its active ingredients include glycerol triesters and excipients that create a lipid film similar to human saliva. This layer reduces dry mouth symptoms and provides lasting mucosal hydration.

How do traditional dry mouth remedies compare to Aquoral in effectiveness?

Traditional treatments like lozenges, gels, and mouth rinses provide varying levels of symptom relief.

Besides lozenges, gels and rinses, topical products like Aquoral are used to relieve symptoms, and some medicines are used, when appropriate, to stimulate saliva production. Saliva substitutes come in sprays, liquids, and pretreated swabs; medicines that stimulate saliva production include pilocarpine (Salagen) or cevimeline (Evoxac). (Source: Sjögren’s, 1986.)

What are the benefits and limitations of lozenges, gels, and rinses?

Lozenges can trigger a salivary reflex and usually provide short-term relief. Topical gels increase moisture on the mucosa but often don’t last long. Mouth rinses may soothe oral tissues but can irritate some people. Compared to these, Aquoral offers longer-lasting lubrication and may be better for people with severe dry mouth.

What do comparative studies show about Aquoral versus traditional treatments?

Studies show that Aquoral improves patient-reported quality of life in dry mouth. Unlike systemic saliva stimulants, which can cause side effects throughout the body, Aquoral works topically for many causes, including therapy-induced and medication-related dry mouth.
