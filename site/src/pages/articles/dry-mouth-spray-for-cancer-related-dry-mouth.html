A dry mouth spray for cancer-related dry mouth: practical relief and prevention

Why cancer treatment causes dry mouth, the risks to watch for, and how a lipid-based spray like Aquoral can restore lubrication and protect the lining of the mouth.

Dry mouth (xerostomia) is a frequent and often disruptive side effect of cancer treatment. When saliva drops, daily activities like speaking, chewing, swallowing and tasting become harder. This article explains why chemotherapy, radiation and many supportive medications can cause dry mouth, what symptoms and complications to watch for, and practical, evidence-based ways to restore lubrication and protect the lining of the mouth. You’ll learn how topical, lipid-based sprays differ from saliva-stimulating medicines and water-based substitutes, how a protective lipid film supports the health of the mouth, and how to use a spray during cancer care. Throughout, we compare topical and systemic options (including pilocarpine and cevimeline) and highlight oral-care measures that lower the risk of tooth decay and infection during and after cancer therapy.

What causes dry mouth during cancer treatment?

Dry mouth during cancer therapy results from reduced salivary function caused by direct gland injury, the effects of chemotherapy, and medication side effects that together reduce lubrication and weaken the mouth’s defences. Radiation to the head and neck can directly damage salivary tissue and ducts; chemotherapy can change saliva composition and irritate the lining of the mouth; and many other drugs taken at the same time (pain relievers, antihistamines, antidepressants) have anticholinergic effects that worsen xerostomia. These processes lead to less saliva, thicker secretions and poorer clearing of the mouth, all of which raise the risk of tooth decay and tissue damage.

Cancer therapies and medications commonly cause dry mouth through three main mechanisms:

  • Direct radiation damage to salivary glands in the treated area, reducing their ability to secrete.
  • Effects of chemotherapy that alter saliva makeup and the resilience of the mouth lining.
  • Medication-related anticholinergic effects, or taking many medicines at once, which blunt salivary reflexes.

Because these pathways differ, tailored approaches, focusing on lubrication, moisture retention or stimulated flow, are needed to relieve symptoms and protect oral health.

How do chemotherapy and radiation lead to xerostomia?

Radiation causes cumulative structural injury to the saliva-producing cells and ducts in the treated area, often producing long-lasting low saliva. Many chemotherapies cause temporary gland dysfunction and inflammation of the mouth lining. Head and neck radiation has the highest rate of persistent dry mouth because the salivary glands are directly exposed; combining treatments increases both severity and duration. In many people, reduced saliva begins during therapy and can persist afterwards, so both immediate symptom control and long-term oral care planning matter.

Studies show that managing cancer therapy-related xerostomia can include a range of tactics, from topical lubricants to other preventive measures.

Cancer therapy-induced xerostomia: management and prevention strategies. Evidence indicates salivary gland hypofunction and therapy-related xerostomia can sometimes be prevented or their symptoms reduced, depending on the cancer treatment. Clinical guidance covers options such as IMRT, amifostine, muscarinic agonist stimulation, oral lubricants, acupuncture and submandibular gland transfer.

What are the symptoms and risks of cancer-related dry mouth?

Common symptoms include ongoing dryness, trouble chewing or swallowing, changes in taste, extra effort when speaking, and a sticky or thick saliva sensation. People often experience poor sleep, discomfort with dentures and reduced appetite because food tastes different or is harder to swallow. Dry mouth increases the risk of tooth decay, oral infections (for example, candida) and oral mucositis, problems that can interfere with nutrition and with completing treatment. Telling your oncology and dental teams early helps you get targeted care.

  • Higher risk of tooth decay because of lost salivary buffering and reduced remineralisation.
  • Increased chance of oral infections and ulcers.
  • Nutrition problems from pain and difficulty swallowing.

These risks make protective lubrication and coordinated oral care essential during cancer therapy.

What is the lipid-based technology behind Aquoral?

Oxidized glycerol triesters (OGT) form a thin, supple lipid film on the lining of the mouth when sprayed. This film mimics natural lipids in the mouth, slows evaporation and restores lubrication. Its structure helps hold water at the surface and reduces friction during speaking and eating, directly addressing the problems caused by xerostomia. Unlike water-based substitutes that evaporate quickly, the OGT lipid film provides sustained moisture retention and less frequent reapplication.

How long does Aquoral’s relief last compared with other treatments?

Aquoral’s topical film typically provides several hours of relief, commonly reported as about four to six hours, which usually means fewer daily applications than water-based sprays. Water-based substitutes often need reapplying every one to two hours, while systemic medicines such as pilocarpine or cevimeline stimulate saliva but can be limited by side effects and by health conditions that rule them out. People on multiple medications or with heart concerns may prefer topical options to reduce exposure to the rest of the body.

How can Aquoral help protect against oral mucositis during cancer therapy?

Oral mucositis, painful inflammation and ulcers of the mouth lining, commonly occurs with chemotherapy and radiation, especially in head and neck cancer. Preventing it matters because severe cases can limit nutrition, increase infection risk and force breaks in treatment. By delivering sustained lubrication and a protective barrier, a lipid-based spray like Aquoral can reduce mechanical trauma from eating and speaking and help keep the lining of the mouth more resilient. Sprays don’t guarantee prevention, but they can lower severity and improve daily comfort when used alongside oral hygiene, pain relief and the mucositis plan from your oncology team.

  • Lubrication: less friction and irritation, so swallowing is easier and eating less painful.
  • Moisture retention: better hydration and barrier function, lowering the risk of cracks and secondary infections.
  • Local film formation: physical protection of ulcer-prone areas, with a potential reduction in mucositis severity and better day-to-day comfort.

What is oral mucositis, and why does prevention matter?

Oral mucositis is a common side effect of chemotherapy and radiation, marked by redness, painful ulcers and breakdown of the lining of the mouth, particularly with head and neck treatments. It can lead to severe pain, weight loss from poor intake, secondary infections and interruptions to cancer therapy. Prevention and early management reduce the need for strong pain medicines, help preserve nutrition and support staying on your treatment schedule.

Oral mucositis in cancer patients: prevention and management update. Oral mucositis (OM) is one of the most frequent adverse effects of chemotherapy and radiotherapy in head-and-neck cancer. Managing OM relies on validated assessment tools, supportive care, symptom relief and patient education. Left unmanaged, OM can cause ulcers, severe pain and swallowing problems that may lead to malnutrition and treatment interruptions.
