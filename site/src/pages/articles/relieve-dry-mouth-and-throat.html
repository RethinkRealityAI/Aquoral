Understanding and relieving dry mouth and throat

The causes of dry mouth and throat, from medications to allergies and mouth breathing, plus remedies, self-care tips and when to seek help.

Experiencing a dry mouth or throat can be more than just an inconvenience; it can significantly affect your overall health. Dry mouth, also known as xerostomia, occurs when the salivary glands don’t produce enough saliva to keep the mouth moist. This often extends to a dry throat, leading to discomfort and potential complications.

Why saliva matters for oral health

  • Aiding digestion
  • Protecting teeth from decay
  • Preventing infection by controlling bacteria and fungi in the mouth
  • Making chewing and swallowing easier

A lack of saliva can lead to numerous issues, including difficulty swallowing, more dental problems and infections.

Dry mouth has both short-term and long-term effects that go beyond discomfort and can affect daily activities such as talking, chewing, swallowing and overall comfort. Poor oral health also affects more than your teeth and gums; it has broader implications for your general health. Neglecting dental care puts you at higher risk of serious health conditions involving your heart, brain, lungs and more, so it’s important to address dry mouth and throat symptoms promptly.

Causes and risk factors

Medications and medical treatments

Certain medications and medical treatments significantly affect the salivary glands, often leading to dry mouth and throat. Many common medications, including antihistamines, decongestants, painkillers, diuretics and antidepressants, can reduce saliva production by interfering with the normal function of the salivary glands.

Cancer therapy, such as chemotherapy and head and neck radiation, is another major contributor. Chemotherapy drugs can alter the composition of saliva and damage the salivary glands temporarily or permanently. Radiation aimed at the head and neck can substantially harm the salivary glands, leading to chronic dry mouth.

Nerve damage from injuries or surgeries involving the head and neck can also disrupt normal saliva production, because nerves stimulate salivary gland activity. When they are damaged, their ability to signal the glands diminishes, causing dryness.

Lifestyle factors: alcohol and tobacco

Alcohol has a dehydrating effect on the body. Its diuretic properties increase urine output, leading to overall dehydration and less saliva. Frequent drinking can make dryness in the mouth and throat worse.

Tobacco use, whether smoking or chewing, also contributes significantly to dry mouth. The chemicals in tobacco irritate the salivary glands and reduce their ability to produce saliva. Smoking further dries out the oral tissues by introducing hot smoke into the mouth, causing irritation and inflammation over time.

These lifestyle factors also contribute to other oral health issues such as tooth decay, hoarseness, gum disease and sore throats. Reducing or eliminating alcohol and tobacco can markedly improve dry mouth symptoms.

Health conditions: diabetes, stroke and Alzheimer’s disease

Diabetes is a prominent example, where fluctuating blood sugar levels affect salivary gland function. High blood glucose increases urination, which causes dehydration and reduces saliva. People with diabetes often have persistent dry mouth that needs careful management to prevent complications like oral infections or tooth decay. See dry mouth and diabetes.

Stroke survivors may experience dry mouth because of medication side effects or nerve damage affecting salivary gland function. A stroke can impair the neural pathways that stimulate saliva, resulting in chronic dryness in the mouth and throat.

Alzheimer’s disease is another significant risk factor. People with Alzheimer’s often have difficulty staying hydrated because cognitive changes affect their ability to drink regularly or recognise thirst. Medications used to manage Alzheimer’s symptoms also frequently reduce saliva.

Understanding these causes helps you address dry mouth effectively, whether through lifestyle changes, careful management of chronic conditions or medical advice when needed.

Sjögren’s disease, an inflammatory condition, is also a common cause of dry mouth. People with it often use saliva substitutes like Aquoral as part of an overall strategy.

The role of saliva: more than just moisture

How snoring and mouth breathing contribute to dryness

Snoring and breathing through the mouth are common causes of dry mouth and throat. During sleep, the constant airflow of mouth breathing causes moisture in the mouth to evaporate, which is often felt as dryness in the mouth or throat on waking.

  • Snoring: disrupts sleep and can cause dryness and irritation in the throat from the vibration of tissues.
  • Mouth breathing: nasal congestion, a deviated septum or chronic allergies can force people to breathe through their mouths, resulting in persistent dryness. See mouth breathing while sleeping.

The many functions of saliva

  • Digestion: Saliva contains enzymes that help break down carbohydrates and fats in the mouth, making food easier to swallow and digest.
  • Oral health: It acts as a natural cleaner, washing away food particles and bacteria that cause plaque and cavities, and helps neutralise acids produced by bacteria.
  • Lubrication: It coats the oral tissues, allowing smooth speech and swallowing.

When there’s not enough saliva, problems can follow, such as difficulty swallowing (dysphagia), changes in taste (dysgeusia) and a higher risk of oral infections such as thrush.

Sjögren’s disease: an autoimmune connection

Sjögren’s disease (also called Sjögren’s syndrome) is an autoimmune disorder that mainly affects the salivary and tear glands, causing dry mouth (xerostomia) and dry eyes (keratoconjunctivitis sicca). It significantly reduces saliva production by attacking the glands that make it.

  • Symptoms: In addition to dry mouth and eyes, people may feel tired, have joint pain and experience swelling.
  • Diagnosis: It often involves blood tests for specific antibodies (e.g., SSA/Ro, SSB/La), imaging of the salivary glands or a biopsy.
  • Impact on oral health: Long-term dryness from Sjögren’s can lead to serious dental problems such as extensive tooth decay, gum disease and oral infections.

Breathing problems from conditions like sleep apnea or chronic nasal congestion should be treated medically to avoid making dry mouth worse. Viral infections, including COVID-19, can also cause temporary dry mouth and tongue discomfort.

External triggers: environmental factors that aggravate dryness

Allergies and saliva production

Allergies can significantly affect saliva production. When the body encounters allergens such as pollen, dust mites or pet dander, it triggers an immune response that often includes inflammation in the nasal passages and throat, which can contribute to dryness.

Some over-the-counter allergy medicines, like antihistamines, can also reduce saliva. While they control sneezing, runny nose and itchy eyes, they often dry the lining of the mouth and throat. The allergic reaction and its treatment together compound the dryness.

Proper diagnosis and treatment of allergies can help. Ask your doctor or pharmacist about allergy treatments that minimise dry mouth. If allergy medicines leave you with significant dry mouth, a saliva substitute like Aquoral may help alongside them.

Viral infections and temporary dry mouth

Viral infections such as the common cold or flu can lead to congestion and mouth breathing during sleep, making the mouth drier. Fever and dehydration often accompany viral illnesses, further decreasing saliva. Together these can make swallowing difficult and increase discomfort.

In most cases, dry mouth from a viral infection is temporary and resolves as the infection clears. While you’re ill, drink fluids frequently, and use a humidifier to keep moisture in the air.

Tips for keeping moist in poor air quality

Poor indoor air can aggravate dry mouth and throat. Common culprits include air conditioning that strips humidity from the air, heating systems that create a dry environment, and pollutants such as dust and smoke.

  • Use a humidifier: Adding moisture to the air helps keep your mucous membranes hydrated.
  • Stay hydrated: Drink water regularly throughout the day.
  • Avoid caffeine and alcohol: Both can dehydrate you.
  • Chew sugar-free gum: Chewing stimulates saliva and helps combat dryness.
  • Clean air filters: Keep heating and ventilation filters clean to reduce dust and particles.
  • Indoor plants: Certain houseplants add humidity while improving air quality.

The vicious cycle: how dry mouth can lead to throat discomfort

Dry mouth and throat often worsen each other, creating a cycle that can be hard to break. Too little saliva dries the mouth, which can lead to a parched throat. That makes swallowing and speaking uncomfortable, causing further irritation. A lack of saliva also lets bacteria and irritants build up, aggravating both.

Hydration and dryness-related discomfort

When you’re dehydrated, the body produces less saliva, intensifying dry mouth and throat. To stay well hydrated:

  • Drink plenty of water: aim for at least 8 glasses a day.
  • Limit caffeine and alcohol: both contribute to dehydration.
  • Eat hydrating foods: fresh fruits and vegetables with high water content help. See nutrition and dry mouth.
  • Use a humidifier: adding moisture to the air can prevent your throat drying out during sleep.

How stress and anxiety affect saliva

Stress and anxiety can worsen dry mouth and throat. The body’s “fight-or-flight” response diverts resources away from saliva production.

  • Reduces salivary flow: stress hormones like cortisol impede the salivary glands.
  • Disrupts acid balance: extra acid from stress further irritates the mouth and throat.

To manage stress and anxiety:

  • Practise mindfulness: meditation or deep-breathing exercises can reduce stress.
  • Exercise regularly: physical activity helps ease anxiety.
  • Seek professional help: a therapist or counsellor can provide personalised strategies.

Managing dry mouth and throat: remedies, self-care and when to seek help

Home remedies

  • Increase hydration: drink water frequently; aim for at least 8 glasses a day.
  • Humidifiers: use one, especially during sleep, to add moisture to the air.
  • Avoid caffeine and alcohol: choose caffeine-free herbal teas or water instead.
  • Sugar-free lozenges or candies: these can stimulate saliva flow.
  • Oil pulling: swishing coconut oil in your mouth for about 15 minutes can help retain moisture.

Practical self-care tips

  • Chew sugar-free gum: chewing stimulates the salivary glands. Choose sugar-free options to avoid tooth decay.
  • Mouth sprays and rinses: products like Aquoral Protective Oral Spray are designed to mimic natural saliva, providing instant relief.
  • Dietary adjustments: eat foods with high water content, such as cucumbers, celery and watermelon. Avoid salty or spicy foods that can worsen dryness.
  • Breathing techniques: practise breathing through your nose rather than your mouth.

When to see a health professional

Persistent dry mouth or throat could signal an underlying condition that needs attention.

  • Medication side effects: many medications, including antihistamines and decongestants, can cause dry mouth. If you suspect a medication, talk to your doctor or pharmacist about alternatives or solutions. Don’t stop a medication on your own.
  • Chronic conditions: diabetes, Sjögren’s syndrome and head and neck cancers often contribute to chronic dry mouth. If you have, or suspect, any of these, seek medical advice.
  • Severe symptoms: if you have severe discomfort, difficulty swallowing, a sore throat lasting more than a week, or signs of infection (like fever), see a health professional.

The impact on dental health: why oral care matters

Dry mouth and throat have significant implications for your teeth. Regular dental check-ups help: dentists can spot early signs of problems caused by dry mouth, such as tooth decay and gum disease, and provide targeted treatment before they become more serious.

Saliva acts as a natural cleanser, washing away food particles and neutralising acids produced by bacteria. When saliva is reduced:

  • Tooth decay: there’s less natural defence against cavities.
  • Gum disease: saliva helps keep oral bacteria in balance; without it, harmful bacteria can multiply, leading to gum infections.
  • Mouth sores: friction from dry tissues can cause painful sores.

Xerostomia can lead to gingivitis and periodontitis. Gingivitis is inflammation of the gums from plaque buildup, which dryness can make worse. Left untreated, it can progress to periodontitis, where the gum pulls away from the teeth, forming pockets that become infected.

  • Hydration: drinking plenty of water helps keep your mouth moist.
  • Chewing sugar-free gum: stimulates saliva.

Regular dental check-ups and diligent oral care can substantially reduce the impact of dry mouth on your teeth and gums.

Taking proactive steps to care for your oral health is crucial, especially with persistent dry mouth and throat. Regular dental check-ups help manage discomfort and prevent problems such as cavities and gum disease. Early action is key to a healthy mouth.
